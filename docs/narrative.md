# The story and the script

The story of a game is not only text. The player meets it through the rules, the places, the objects, the sounds, and
the words. This document gives the order of the story work, the roles, and the documents. Even a game with no
dialogue needs the first four steps.

## 1. The order of the work

| Step | Phase | Output | Who writes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Premise | concept | One sentence: who, wants what, against what, in which world | the human |
| 2. Theme | concept | The question that the game asks the player (not the answer) | the human |
| 3. The player role | concept | Who the player is in the story, and what the player can change | the human, with the designer |
| 4. Mechanics that carry the theme | concept, pre-production | Which rules make the player feel the theme | the human and the owners |
| 5. The world | pre-production | The story bible: places, history, rules of the world, factions, what is true and what people believe (`templates/story-bible.md`) | the human; sessions research and check |
| 6. The characters | pre-production | For each main character: what they want, what they need, their flaw, their voice (three sample lines) | the human |
| 7. The structure | pre-production | The beat outline mapped to the progression of the game (levels, missions, hours) (`templates/script-outline.md`) | the human, with the coordinator |
| 8. The script | production | Scenes, dialogue, barks, notes, item texts, menus | the human writes the key scenes; a narrative session drafts the rest in the voice of the bible; the human edits all of it |
| 9. The world tells the story | production | Environmental storytelling: props, traces, light, sound, the reactions of systems | the World and Art owners, from the bible |
| 10. The test | each milestone | A playtest with comprehension questions: what happened, who is who, what do you want to do next | the runner and the human |

## 2. Rules

1. **The human owns the voice.** The premise, the theme, the main characters, the key scenes, and the ending come from
   the human. A session can draft barks, item texts, and variations in the voice that the bible defines; the human
   edits each line that a player reads.
2. **Mechanics and story agree.** When the rules tell the player one thing and the story tells another (ludonarrative
   dissonance), players notice. For each pillar, write how the rules and the story both serve it.
3. **Show it in the world.** Environmental storytelling works when the world building, the level design, and the
   details fit together. Give the World and Art owners the story bible, and a list of the traces that each place must
   show.
4. **Past the first ideas, also for the story.** Write the five most obvious premises for your setting and your genre
   (the first ideas of everybody), and make sure that your premise is none of them.
5. **One source of truth.** The story bible is the reference for all sessions. A change of a fact changes the bible
   first.
6. **A text budget.** Count the words for each scene and each level. Long text in a game with fast play is rarely read.

## 3. What AI sessions do well here

- **Consistency checks:** each new text against the story bible (names, dates, rules of the world, the voice).
- **Cliché checks:** a list of the phrases and the tropes that a text uses too often, for the human to change.
- **Variations:** many short variations of a bark or an item text, for the human to select and edit.
- **Research:** facts about real places, jobs, history, and language, with sources.
- **Tools:** a dialogue format, a script export to the game data, a word count, a check that each line has a speaker
  and a trigger.

## 4. What to avoid

- The first output as final text: it is often generic, and players notice it.
- A story that does not change what the player does.
- Long exposition at the start: let the player play first, and give the world through play.
- Facts that change between scenes because no bible exists.
